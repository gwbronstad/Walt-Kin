Sep. 12, 1963 Letter from Anne Whaling to Pat and Walt

Apt 837, University Terrace 
Ann Arbor, MI 48104

3320 Daniels
Dallas, TX 75205

Dear Walton and Pat


My hasty postcard was no proper acknowledgment of your letter with news of your neighbors and your discovery of trees and grass in the Arboretum (which only a Texan can fully appreciate. ) You did not say whether the neighbors were from your department or some other, but they sound quite typical of a big graduate school, and by this time you have probably met a good many others, also very strangely assorted. Louis Weil, here for a visit, told me that Harvard collects an assortment as a matter of policy, that is if they have a choice between two students of equal ability, they give the preference to the non-Harvard student. Perhaps this is fairly generally true in major schools. I’m delighted, and the sensation of something like intellectual claustrophobia, which troubled me while you were in Arlington is beginning to diminish.


Even at Caltech people are concerned about the intellectual effect of isolation of students under present conditions. David Reisman studied up this issue, along with a lot of others, in his visit last spring. Of course, a great deal of what students say serves no real purpose except to reinforce your own confidence as you see how little there is to it. But after all the important new ideas aren’t yet in books, they will be emerging, dimly, in graduate schools as people talk, wherever they can get a minute at lunch or over coffee.


To come back to present realities, it had already occurred to me that “coffee“ would be the sort of entertainment. Pat would do most often at first, and I wondered whether you would like recipes for things to go with it.) I’m already losing control of the pronoun in the attempt at joint address) the sunset breakfast book may have something, but I haven’t used it enough to make suggestions. Helen Corbett has a few, but I haven’t tried them. I’ll try to find other others if you would be interested.


The cooking, I realize, should go on a footnote or something, since it doesn’t concern Walton, but Caltech, I’m sure should interest you both, though in different ways and degrees. I spent much of the visit being an invalid, but I heard enough of what went on to feel again the tremendous life of the place. Ward [Whaling, Anne Whaling’s brother, Cal Tech Physics Professor] had just written the annual division report and he told me about the infrared techniques in astronomical observation thought up by a geologist, and started with a home made telescope, now becoming a big thing, about other work in astrophysics and gravitational theory (Feinmann’s current preoccupation) about Murray Gell-Mann’s work on fundamental particles (his concept of the “eightfold way“ sounds like an oriental religion doctrine) and so on.


Even on Labor Day weekend, things do not stop. The Sunday morning, the presidents secretary (who was at the office) called Ward to say that a visitor from Italy wanted to know about their work on gravitation. Ward gave him a summary, and he later got Feinmann on the phone for half an hour. Monday morning Ward got a call from a colleague at the lab and rushed off to “sit” for him all afternoon. (No one is allowed to work on the machine alone – too dangerous.) By Tuesday new people were coming in, one from South Africa, and one the director of the Greenwich, observatory and Royal astronomical, here to spend a year working on nuclear physics.


And always, I got an impression of freedom as nearly complete as any man ever has. I suppose, and an administration designed to set these people free to work. Ward was talking about their four theoretical physicist, Gel Mann, Feinmann, Christi and Leverett Davis. They are always thinking, he said, and they take an original view of anything. They are sometimes so unconventional that it “ embarrasses the institute“. He added. But no one considers this important. Least of all the Institute which they make famous. These people are committed to finding out the truth about something, and absolutely nothing else matters – what people hear, always thought, what people want to think, who they want, and their own interest to force others to think – none of this is of the slightest importance. And as for the degree of personal autonomy possible, Ward is still driving his 54 Chevrolet, the full professor. He and M. L. like to look at and rent paintings, and if they find a painting they want to live with, I feel sure they will buy it and continue to drive the old car. Ward actually rides a bicycle to school anyway.


You can imagine how wonderful this seems to someone just out of Arlington. And Surely, there is some element of this at Michigan somewhere though the great size of the place, state control, and many other things may obscure it. I’m very curious naturally, about the people in your department – Dr. Melton and the others you have met – and about the counseling, the courses and all the rest. But I know that describing all this is a major effort and very time-consuming. I’ll take for granted the usual dim view and inevitable disappointment, and all the incredible variety and proliferation of misgivings to be expected in this situation., But I know that this can change and Will, if you are given and even chance. I will not ask questions, but simply “stand and wait.“


(One final point from the Caltech visit Ward said that he did know of J Russo, who was on the list of students I saw. Ward said he wasn’t much of a student, but very social, involved in student activities and would make a good salesman. He said he was talking to Lebowitz, student president at a cocktail party for David Reese, and learned that both Lebowitz at Berkeley and Russo we’re going into psychology, a rare thing hitherto at Cal Tech. Ward also said that Caltech sends a lot of students to Michigan in physics and chemistry. You may socially meet some of them.)


There is so much more about Reese‘s visit left (I thought Pat might like to try the lonely crowd) and they talk I had (socially but we talked Shop intermittently) with one of the junior members of the Stanford English Department (I was amazed and much pleased to find that things I have thought of and tried last summer where exactly what is done at Stanford and Berkeley– the textbook I’ve cherished in the printed cover) is the one they use at Berkeley? (it doesn’t Joep the Norton anthology, which is the survey type, and both should give you a very respectable resource for beginning in English literature – even if you haven’t read them, you know where to look.) But this letter is too lengthy now – you may not even have time to read it.


Be sure to let me know if there is anything I can do to help Pat in the job hunting. I hope so much this works out quickly and pleasantly, and that all this stage of bewildering newness and general wretchedness will soon be over. I will be thinking of you.


Anne


P. S. For Pat


The piece of gray green wool I sent is a rim remnant, 2 yards, 54 inches enough for a dress. I was told. Not for a full shirt, but you wouldn’t want that in this material anyway. The cotton is 39 inches I think, and there are 4 yards of it, which you can use for anything (a tablecloth, possibly, or whatever else occurs to you – do you remember Dorothy Kelly’s red “bedspread“ in the wall?) At least you can practice sewing on it. I thought these colors rather looked like you.


I came home from California with many notes on him. L. Superlative cooking – on simple menu, she used and table setting. I’ll save these in case you may be interested later. The textbook I put in the box for you. (a duplicate I had) is a very good introductory one on in case you want to join in the studying. I imagine you will find plenty to keep you busy. Be sure to let me know if there is anything more I can send


P. S. For Walton


(I talked to Ward about leaving a note with my Will that I wanted you to have some of my books – he thought it a splendid idea, but said it would be even better to give you some now please accept them in the spirit.


I’m afraid you are rather appalled by the size of the box of books, but I think you will Understand my reason for choosing them. [Erich] Fromm and [Paul] Tillich , because both in someway have spoken for me – not as psychologist and theologian, but as observer of our social order. You do not need to look further than Fromm’s “universal ethics“ (in no way limited to Fromm) for the theoretical structures on which you your own characteristic reaction rest – I would have told you if there had been time, that you might consider as particular issues arise, what justice, generosity, and truth make of the “socially immanent“ layers with which we are so effectively brainwashed. If in our time men has suddenly managed to confront the inconsistencies involved in segregation (which even a few years ago was as unquestioned as any other) perhaps some further growth may be possible – not in my time but in yours. Next, please tell me if you wish me to write again exactly what will be acceptable. I have spent more hours than you would believe possible writing, and rewriting this in an effort to meet the requirements implied, I thought, in your letter, but I am very much bewildered and unaware of my ground. It would be immensely easier and more natural for me to write to you and to Pat individually (this is the custom, I know) and if necessary, put both letters in the same envelope, but you must tell me what she prefers. If you think it better for me not to write at all, I will just remind you that my belief in the principle of Veritas remains. It has been more severely tested in these last weeks than ever before and the darkness still seems impenetrable, but the hope is there. And I will add as [T. S.] Elliot does (with all Dante‘s magnificent behind his work) “Sovena vos“ (be mindful).

Letter's Context

2026-11-22

Anne's grim phrase:, "I spent much of the visit being an invalid" still alarms; self-centeredly, I suspect her state reflects her stress over her mentor role in my academic transition and urgent need for my success in that transition in the face of signs that I was troubled by my choice.


Summer of '63 I was taking the last, required courses for my B. S. in Psychology, finding it hard to stir up motivation. I had little taste for more visits with Anne despite her entreaties. Anne had painted a bright, exciting picture of the academic world, but I had found nothing exciting in the academic achievements in the effort to understand ourselves. Contemporary eforts seemed no more coherent, no more true, than the achiements of the ancient world.


I no longer remember a boxfull of books, but I do recall the heavy reading burden of Michigan courses, more books, not of my chodng, would have had little more than decorative value.
