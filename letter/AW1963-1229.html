Dec. 29, 1963 Letter from Anne Whaling to Walton Bronstad

Apt 837, University Terrace 
Ann Arbor, MI 48104

3320 Daniels
Dallas, TX 75205

Dear Walton,


I found your postcard when I got in this evening, and I just have to tell you how happy and proud I am that you have come through a first semester of graduate school with the sort of record you describe. If you can do that, coming from Arlington, and carrying the weight of depression I saw when you left, you have the ability and strength to do anything, and I should think that even you would begin to realize it I know that you are perplexed and troubled about the direction to take, but surely – once you have caught up on your sleep and had a few days change of occupation you will find that you feel much more sure of yourself. And I believe that the depression will begin to seem less formidable, whatever the causes you should begin gradually to feel more able to deal with them. And you may even begin (for the first time) to know what I have meant by saying that there is really nothing wrong at all – nothing wrong with you that is except too severe restriction (a concept of restriction derived from your environment) that prevented the unfolding and growth the organism required. This will come, if you understand what is involved – I do not know how, but this does not really matter so much as long as you choose life rather than death, or the death in life of trying to live at levels too low for you.


There is nothing adequate I can say. Perhaps later, I can add something, but now I can only remind you of what you know already, that this is a matter of life, knowing that what I could do contributed to life and death for me too.


(Marginalium 1. When and how do you apply for renewal of the NSF grant?, How would a change of field affect that?)


(Marginalium 2. If you ever have occasion to talk to someone about the depression, please tell them that a Yale PhD, who taught you (over several years) came to this conclusion, the university should be helpful.)


I hope you can talk to someone there during the holidays – possibly the best time to find them free (at Caltech people seemed to be all over the campus, much as usual.) of course I couldn’t (and wouldn’t) think of making any specific suggestion as I still know nothing, really, of the circumstances, general or particular. I’m at not sure whether your phrase “the brand I’ve associated with now.“ means a field you are supposed to be specializing in “experimental?“, a particular person you are supposed to work with, or (possibly) the Department in general (though this seemed so varied and extensive that I don’t see how this could fit.) I remember that when they sent the list of students last June the fields were put down as tentative, so some change here surely couldn’t be surprising.


I could make a few general observations – if you are detaching yourself from one instructor to work with someone else, please remember to be tactfull, and reasonably kind about it – little as you think so, this can be a fairly severe blow to the instructor in many cases – they also have feelings, even if they seem secure professionally, and don’t apparently need student support of this kind (they often do) I might also remind you again of the danger of sounding unnecessarily negative. Somehow there is a great difference between saying that you’re not interested in something and saying that you’re still finding your way, and still looking for something (or saying that you do not want to work with someone on something and saying that you would like to try something else.) I apologize for repeating the very obvious, now it occurred to me that you might not have thought of all the bearings of this, and it would certainly be natural if your resentment at having been put through an ordeal and your dissatisfaction with the field, somehow showed in your manner, I can imagine you looking dreadfully stern and severe. (perhaps you have ever since those first days that were blacked out out of your letters you never mention meeting these people or conferring about courses) – if you would just permit yourself to smile at them once, just a little, I think it would strike them as much as it did me when I saw it for the first time. They know now that you are a capable student, but they could not have known what sort of person you are. (No one does, of course, but I meant this is the simplest practical sense)


And please try to tell them enough, your habit of concealing what you think and feel is so strong that it makes things very difficult sometimes. Their advice can’t possibly be sound if you withhold the evidence they need – their conjectures can’t serve your purposes. (I have been wrong often enough myself – the conviction eventually came through in someway, but it took time, which they don’t have.)


Perhaps you will find that you can take a little more time to make a final decision. You have had such a little time to get acquainted with the academic world that you can’t judge very clearly the professional significance of steps you might take. That is what your advisors are for, of course, but you would be saved some embarrassment and uncertainty, if you could see some of the issues clearly yourself. This sort of thing comes in large part through association with other students, the one most serious gap in your preparation. I imagine that you will begin to get acquainted a little now, and if Pat understands how important this is, I’m sure you can arrange things to permit conversation (casual academic, not social with wives) a little. (Again at Caltech I heard discussion of the importance of this for students and faculty as well. The graduate students have to go out of the building for their coffee so they will meet and talk – the faculty have it brought to their offices, but Willie Fowler(head of Kellogg’s) wants to make a vacant room into a coffee center so they can exchange ideas. The same thing is true of the Wisconsin enzymology Institute, where where Mary Lou’s aunt is working; she said attendance at the coffee break was mandatory. This is the only way to keep up with developments.)


But I know you will overcome this handicap as you have others. Perhaps you have already been made aware of the importance of these professional considerations – it, just that I am haunted by your mention of the university of Texas medical school. If you could even think of that (or or for that matter of Denton’s) so recently as last, October, my anxiety can’t really be considered excessive.


All I have, of course, is your brief sensible statement that you want to talk to people about the possibilities, and all of this may be beside the point, but I hope you will forgive me for sending it on. I would not weary you with so much – a sort of bracketing a target with fire if I knew exactly what the target was. I do know how to come to the point, and I hope that someday I can prove it to you. As it is, I just try everything that occurs to me.


There is so much that I want to tell you – about my wonderful visit in California (the guest for Christmas dinner included a young MD research fellow at UCLA, joining the faculty, whose interest is the relation of endocrine to brains, though he has been working recently on population control. (Ward said all the medical people he knows are working on this) M.L.’s mother was expecting a visitor who is wife of the head of the mental retardation project established by Kennedy. And so on and on. For some research, there was much more talk of people in medicine and psychology than usual – perhaps I just have this on my mind, but it seemed to turn up at every point in the conversations.


I also had a delightful evening with a Yale friend, the musicologist who first told me of Dr David’s importance – she’s working in English, actually, teaching at Montana in residence at the Huntington library for the winter quarter to do a book on Skelton. Ward and others had her over for dinner the night before I left.


I will stop and get this off – just in case some suggestion buried in all this might be useful when you talk to people next week. I’ll try to do better later.


Please tell Pat the compilations are for her too. I know that there has been much new for her in his first semester and she must have done beautifully too. I will try to write to her about the Caltech visit – I never visit there without thinking that this is exactly the sort of thing she would enjoy very simple and natural, and attractive, and intensely alive. In spite of the furious pace of the work there is a great deal of sociability – people are in some special sense gay. And you would love it


(Marginalium 3)


Because there is autonomy – people can be themselves, can make a life that seems good to them. There is never this sort of freedom in any other world I have seen. Please hold on, whatever you decide about feels, until you can learn what this is like. You will not be in danger of taking a step backwards once you have seen it. Please try to look all around you, and out, not just originally ahead, or down, or back. All my loyalty and hope,


Anne


Letter's Context

2026-11-22

Fear was my academic engine. Fear of not measuring-up when Mother voiced her indignation after being interviewed for my admission to 6th grade: the Oak Knoll principal voiced low expectations for students from rural schools. Fear of not keeping up in the top level of grade stratified Riverside Junior High. Fear of discomforting Mother by having poor grades when I entered the same (Birdville) school system in which she taught. Fear of losing my Chance-Vought co-op position whilst an undergraduate. Fear of losing my NSF fellowship at Michigan, finally, fear of getting sucked back into the Army during the Vitnam war.
