Nov. 2, 1963 Letter from Anne Whaling to Walton Bronstad

Apt 837, University Terrace 
Ann Arbor, MI 48104

3320 Daniels
Dallas, TX 75205

Dear Walton,

It may seem strange to say that I feel greatly cheered by a letter, which is so obviously written de profundis, but this actually sounds more normal than anything you have said or written since that Monday afternoon (August 4) when you came in for the last time. Please do not give any thought now to what I have written (a very faint echo really of my tearing anxiety) – I hope you will keep the letters and someday we can talk again and unravel some of the tangles – now nothing matters except your immediate problem. And do not be concerned about my project – a first, simple answer is far easier than you probably think, as I expected much this sort of reaction, if not, precisely the form and direction of thought you describe. There is much that I can, and should (should have said long ago) say that is not simple, but this can be added a little at a time, and nothing would help as much with the project as knowing that I can reach you. There is a quite indescribable relief involved – I think I did not quite realize until I saw this how frightened I had been by your polite, beautifully written letters, as impersonal as the wallpaper, without a word about a feeling or thought, or any reaction to the shock of transition inevitable under the circumstances.


But I will come back to all this – at the moment there is one overwhelmingly urgent thing to say – please don’t do anything that involves a change of plan while you feel this way. It is very possible, as I have suggested before, that, as you find your way, the direction will be out of this field. (if you can call anything a field, that is such an ill defined territory, including so much that seems, to me inconsistent.) but you have not seen enough yet, I think, to make this sort of choice, or to know exactly what is possible and where and how. This will take care of itself, but you are entirely right and thinking that proposing a change now would put you in a very difficult position. The change from E. E. To another field, obviously did not suggest in stability to anyone who considered the evidence, because regardless of how you felt, you had stayed with engineering long enough to prove that you could hold a steady course under very adverse conditions. Then your decision when you made it, was handled by all the objective evidence it was possible to gather, the interest test results, test scores, the observation of the older people you worked with (differing radically in background, and in the kind of opportunities to observe, but in agreement on the potential – through all, I think, anxious (certainly [Dr.]Segal and [Dr.]Cheavius) individually indicated this) because they could see the depression). No one would hold you forever to a field you knew so little about to begin with – it would not surprise me to know that most of the people concerned rather expected a change (or rather, specialization). I think that I did myself – I know that I had spoken of this possibility. But any move of the sort now would look to anyone as if you were simply cracking under the strain.


There may be quite objective considerations which you have not mentioned. These could be (1) personal some development affecting Pat (and unexpected pregnancy (please tell me if this is so) severe home sickness, fear that she cannot do the academic work needed, inability to meet Michigan’s interest requirements, pressure from her family (did Betty come?) Are all sorts of other things, including the reaction to prolonged irritation over relative solitude, which has wrecked so many graduate students) or even conceivably, your anxiety about your parents or something of the kind. Knowing how you habitually respond to any demand made on you, I realize that any of these might lead you to try to change your plans. (the fact that you can even think of coming back to Texas, suggested something of the sort to me as soon as I read your letter). Or there could be (2) negative academic evidence. If you have just finished two five week courses, perhaps you received grades on these which were not satisfactory or perhaps papers or reports or test have discouraged you. I can say to this two or three things,: first, you should not be too sure you can evaluate them correctly. Any student making the transition from Arlington to Michigan is facing terrific difficulties, and no one knows this quite as well as the very experienced men who considered your application and responded as they did. They are so much more discerning (I learned at Yale) than you think, and it would not surprise me to find, in a case, like this, that they expected and allow for some failure of various kinds at first. Certainly this was true at Yale – I did very poorly at first in two of my three courses, but the difficulty I was contending with were obvious (I did not have to tell anyone, not having your composure and control) and a little of the work showed possibilities when I asked Karl Young  (in April!) Whether I should come back, the answer was yes. The second year was better, but still weak, so much so, I thought, that I felt far behind everyone else. But again, they gave me a University Fellowship, one of the largest available then, in Karl Young’s phrase “a vote of confidence“– not for what I had done, but for what it was fairly obvious to them, I might do if I could overcome my fears and so on). On paper and by all the measurements I knew, it was one thing that their judgment overruled all this. I understood at last, and I even realized, at the end (after the thesis!) That all the testing and observation was perfectly right and that this was the thing I was interested in) I did not intend to worry you with all this painful personal experience from an academic world for less competitive than yours now, but the principal does apply. I’ve often noticed and tried to warn you about your tendency to grade yourself, or two calculate the results and possibilities as you went along. This is not just paralyzing – it can be fatal. (again I could offer my experience, but won’t) especially, I can’t resist, adding, if you listen to yourself as you talk so much energy and attention are diverted from what you are doing that it can be disastrous. (If I can renounce, Hope and give up completely, then go on and finally just because one is supposed to go through the motion of trying it usually turns out well – that has happened so many times now that I can count on the results!)


If there is nothing concrete and obvious that you have not told me under (1) and (2) there are two other obvious and inevitable possibilities (3) one is primarily physical – this strain is appalling and in such a case as yours, doubly so – anything so infinitely sensitive will take simply fearful punishment from anxiety, loss of sleep, exhaustion of every sort (including the effects that long and dreadful summer) all of this compounded by the effort of concealment. It is also possible that there is something wrong – I have often thought and watched you that you simply were not feeling well, though you might not be aware of anything you could recognize as a symptom and therefore just ignored it. Things usually have to be acute before patient (or doctor) catches on but very good ones. (doctors that is) are sometimes very observant and imaginative. (I am not convinced that anything so severe as the attack of pain in class last summer [kidney infection] is to be dismissed so lightly as your doctor in Arlington thought. I have often remembered that and understand).


There are always those marginal possibilities, (and I hope and trust you will go and ask someone about them simply as one phase of an investigation of your problem.) but it is likely, of course, that you were just dreadfully tired and badly damaged by prolonged nervous strain. Then there is, finally, (4) the depression (immediately acute, and long-term chronic), which must have been obvious to the men in your department, and which has been a matter of the deepest anxiety to me ever since I first talked to you. There is something wrong in many things, perhaps – but because you did not feel you could honor anyone with your confidence, and because no one felt it possible to ask questions under these circumstances, no one could help. We could, and did, try to change some of the conditions that were clearly disastrous, and the evidence that there was the right general direction came when you responded, at least intermittently, and the depression lifted quite visibly, you may not have been aware of a change, but it was very wonderful to see – your color. This was very striking. I’ve never seen this reaction before) brightened you talked more easily, you would smile, or even laugh softly, you would talk quite naturally about something you were working on and for moments at least you were interested. Then the depression would close in again. Since I saw you so little, and you told me so little, the circumstances, I could not know exactly what gave the result. I observed (one thing I did notice was the moment of relief from depression frequently accompanied the moments of companionship, with men in the Department or even outside – the day you came in after playing chess with one of the math teachers – and almost always after some academic success, I noticed the brightening.) The reasonable inference was that given a chance to move further in the same direction you might gradually be able to throw off more of the depression, perhaps, as things improved , to understand the causes yourself, even if you would not share your trouble with anyone else. I know, of course, that there was a very great risk involved and undertaking the early stages of graduate work, it is killing, even under favorable circumstances, but sometimes a risk is worth taking, necessary. Certainty the people who appraised your testing score and general record thought so of the risk in general and the inadequate background, they could not know of the depression and lack of confidence. (though I didn’t use this term in writing to Michigan.) but I think that if they had, they might have thought as I did (and I should guess the men in your department shared this feeling) that the best way out was to meet the difficulty head on. Otherwise, you would never know what you could do.


There was the general view I had, but then there were the severe attacks of depression – the recurring set of symptoms (which I tried more than once to list for you) that I saw (A) when you move to Arlington, (B) at the very beginning of the summer between semesters and (C) finally just before you left. I had realized whenever I saw you after August 4, that you were not yourself ( though Pat seemed elated, as in the other two periods) had the circumstances made impossible to speak or even to give you my full attention, and when I finally spoke to you for just a minute, I. recognize this same pattern again. All that I could do was to write the messages I left in my notes and then try to whether the serious depression I have ever felt myself. I know that if you faced that journey to a completely strange land and anything as grueling as graduate work in the state of mind, I recognized from the painful experience of seeing it twice before, it would take absolutely superhuman strength to pull through. I know that you had it I had come to know, gradually that you had endure the things that broke most people, but even so I dreaded it for you more than I can say. And when you wrote, as you did without a trace of feeling, and without any reference to meeting the people in your department, the strange blindness suggested, among other things, a state of shock.


If you are willing to talk to the people who are concerned for you, so much more than you think can be done. I cannot know where you should start, but consider the possibilities in the academic people. (do you have an advisor?) Did you ever meet and talk to Dr. Melton? He had been over your application and was obviously informed and interested), or possibly your neighbor the anthropologist student and your department are competitors, busy concealing Themselves and their difficulties so that you would find it hard at first I should think to know them well. If you consult a doctor (any brand) he will doubtless be thoroughly familiar with graduate student problems, and if the depression (now or later) seems to warrant help of the specialist in such things, they would know who to suggest. I think it was in the school general information bulletin that I read a detailed description of the services for students. They probably do try to help, and they might as well help you as anyone else. (But please remember you will have to put your case before them in reasonably complete form. Otherwise, they will simply guess and be wrong. You are not typical of anything)


As for Phillips and McCain, I do not know how you feel – you never spoke of talking to them about the things that troubled you, and if they keep if they know very little, it might be difficult to write. (I have not seen any of these people I saw and nodded to Dr. Segel from a distance at a faculty meeting, but otherwise I have not left our building.) They could not possibly feel there was any betrayal of trust if for some reason you could not go on there, but I think they would regard the proposal to come back to Texas to medical school as a symptom of a serious crack up. If you could just withhold this part of it, you could write it length without being exhausted and discouraged so on without alarming anyone unduly. (I remember McCain: remark about not showing your face in Texas“ if you flunked out.) perhaps they feel, as I did, that not hearing something like this is “alarming!“


You have asked what I think, but perhaps you know already what I would say. You sound strained to the point of breaking, and nothing can possibly seem bright when you feel that way. Please don’t let yourself decide anything just yet you cannot possibly judge the academic problem fairly – wait until people have had a chance to observe a little longer and know you better. You’ll just have to trust them a little, if not completely. The plans and judgments that were made last spring, quite objectively, by people with a great deal of experience, are a far safer ground for decision than what you feel now. If you are at a great disadvantage and have grounds to be made up, people there will know that, and if you have the courage to hold on, I think it’s overwhelmingly likely that they will help you. Gradually, you will find that other students feel the same way (who you least expected


As for the interest – you could not possibly feel anything like a normal interest in anything right now. I would not give that a moment, fault. If you do find that you are very strongly drawn to biology (the branch of things) you can almost certainly find your way into it, but only on the other side of what you’re doing now. In that case, it would be not just a change (instability) are a step backward (returning to Texas) but going ahead into something specialized. Medical school seems to be much further from what you can what you are suited to than this – assuming that you could cover the prerequisites now the grueling ordeal of the work, even in Texas, I have observed and the revolting aspect of it (beginning with gross anatomy and continuing of actual clinical work will the thoroughly revolting live specimens – Dudley, who is a sample, thick skin, normal type he just decided that he wants to teach!“ Would be terribly difficult for you, at least until you are completely out from under the shadow of the depression. After you have survived a year of this, and when people there can know you, there it might be different, but even than not at the U of Texas middle school*(whatever the reason may be for you thinking of Texas, I think I know that if you simply gave up and came back, you would never be free of a feeling of guilt and failure, even if you meant southwestern here, I’ve known the Dean in charge of admission and know his view on motivation he would not be sympathetic.


I do think that mid schools are more likely than any other to heavily factors like uncertainty about interest in change people can’t unless they are completely on track. *it is very low ranking academically, even by Texas standards has been for years – and Galveston is a perfectly dreadful place. My parents were sit there for a few months, just before my father retired. Nothing would induce me to go there for any reason.


If you came because Pat insisted if you came back with a good degree to help raise the level of things, that might be different – but if you just stay here, you cannot help. (I am surrounded by excellent people who are so limited limited in experience in observation that they can do nothing but perpetuate things as they are.)


However, you feel right now, you know that you have abilities and qualities that are very rare permit. (remember that I had nothing whatever to do with the NSF fellowship, if you’re thinking that this is simply my view) you know this and when the strain is over you will remember it.


Perhaps by the time this reaches you the crisis(this one, at least) will be over and you will wonder what I am so much concerned about. I have talked at this length, partly out of sheer relief at having you sound thoroughly human once more, and pleased because you will let me feel that I am helping a little. I will mail this in the morning so you’ll know that I haven’t sacrificed my paper, and I think I will send it special delivery since you spoke of writing to McCain and Phillips this weekend. Perhaps some of this will be relevant (this isn’t strong enough please don’t let them know you have a thought of this.)


I promise to keep the reply within bounds if you will write again. Please let me help in someway. And please let me sign just once as an old friend may, With love.


Anne.

The only thing I know that the major attacks of depression(a, b, and c) had as a common factor is increased isolation (working at home, eliminating time spent in conversation) as response to demands listed as (1) though you would see it as your own decision of (there is no control is effective as that exerted indirectly by what is seemed to be weakness and helplessness) for him. The conclusion is strenghtened by another common factor, the very marked elation. I saw in Pat at all three periods. How, of course, factors (2) and (3) may be involved, as well as some elements unknown to me. Extending into (4). I should add one more thing. One of the things I wrote, but did not send was a note on my observation of the symptoms at closed () not knowing that there was no “outside“ cause and assuming that you were governed by some requirement some course of force, I ask you to consider these points: first, that nothing which has a massively destructive effect on a personality can be good. (all right). Even though it has the strongest Social sanction (consider the parallel of the inquisition). And, second that no one who cares for you could ever ask of you anything which has such an effect.


Surely, there is someone there who can clarify things for Pat and make it possible for her to see what is involved. (I know that she wants more than anything else to do what is best for you.) But if, in the nerve-racking uncertainty of the moment, you also are overwhelmed by a longing for return to the simple world of high school(or Arlington) remember that you had led the life of a co-op student in every possible respect, incomplete conformity with the patterns of life, Pat, New, for more than a year before you (RI) ever spoke of the possibility of a change, and I certainly do not need to remind you of the desperate need for a radical change, evident in declining grades, and in general deterioration, that we all saw then. Please do not let anything distort your saying and soberly, accurate judgment, not even the weariness and wretchedness of things as they are now. Things will change (“everything is in flux)“ and just how will not really matter as long as you do not consider knowingly, take a step backwards. Please try to care for yourself – if you will, then things will be well for those who depend on you and won’t above all things, for you to be happy.


Letter's Context

2026-11-18

Over the years, Anne and I exchanged many visits: beginning with my Freshman visit to her Arlington College, English Department office, she to my parents E Loraine home to meet my parents and later to dine with Pat and me at our Sylvania Street apartment; I alone or with Pat to dine or meet Anne's brother Ward or to attend a movie, or before a required graduate school test at SMU nearby her 3320 Daniels, Dallas home she inherited from her Perkins Theology Dean Father.


On the last semester I had given to EE, I had burdened myself with a heavy course load; the department chair Dr Solis, had to approve the load and warned me I'd have to "retread my brain". I made good grades, but felt burned out. I wanted something different and Anne, with whom I had taken many literature courses was supportive. Of the books she had loaned me, William H. Whyte's Organization Man and David Riesman, Nathan Glazer, and Reuel Denney's Lonely Crowd garnered most of my interest, but Arlington had no Sociology program; Psychology was as close as Arlington got.


The letter Anne referred to was written 63 years ago; I don't have it, I mailed it to Anne and regretably, even if the letter did not perish, I have no way of connecting. Anne Whaling  , born 30 March 1914, died 29 May 1998. But I can guess based on the content of Anne's letter and my general recollection of my transition of 1962 - 1963 from LTV co-op student in Electrical Engineering at Arlington State College to University of Michigan graduate student in Psychology.


Initially the transition was full of anticipation: study man, not electrical manipulatations. Personality, perception, development, learning, social relations, not resistors, capacitors, inductors, transistors, or networks. What I had imagined to be a rewarding feast became a compulsory eating contest. I had to whip through the degree requirements and stay on calendrical academic track. What looked delectable on the academic menu became serving after serving of frustrated human efforts. So far as we know, electrons have no reason to lie; humans often have the best of reasons not to tell the truth, even to themselves.


By summer's start, 1963, I was very unhappy with what I had done; I'd abandoned a relatively secure B.S. in E.E and gone to a path with far more uncertainty and far less remuneration. Arlington academic people had vouched for me so that I could apply for an NSF fellowship and to the universities of Minnesota, Wisconsin and Michigan. Anne and I had persuaded Pat and my family that the Psychology path was the correct one; how could I go back?


I'd had an earfull of Anne's counsel and would hear more only out of burdened politeness. It could have been depression, it felt more like "self-condemned man stagers to his fate".


So after the better part of a semester of the much more demanding work at Michigan, I felt ready to shed the appearance of "all is well" and complain of the miserable situation I'd placed myself in. Anne had few options outside of "eternal optimism", a set of pathways of addressable problems, and a nautical, "steady as she goes."



Family Search tells me of my relationship to Anne Whaling: she is my 8th cousin twice removed. One William Browne, 1623 – 21 January 1701 is a common ancestor. Of note, in 1678 tithables list for Surry County shows Wm. Browne with 6 tithable slaves.
